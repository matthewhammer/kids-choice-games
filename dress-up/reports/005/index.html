Always be knolling

What did the two full-screen designers, 🎵 Music and 🗺️ World design, do the same way that the rest of Dress Up's panels do not, and should every panel move to it? Read from the game's code at 0.0.204 (every row each tab declares, and which a player sees in each place), with pictures of the panels as they are and mockups of what they could be (^ticket(279)).

Short answers.

  1. The two designers share one way of laying out a panel, 13 choices in all: one way in and out, the rest of the page cleared, a slim bar, cards in a grid, boxes within boxes, kinds told apart by a faint tint, fewer words, and a row on one line. The world designer adds the game itself as its first card. Each choice was asked for along the way (tickets 159, 210, 224, 226, 231, 250), and none of it has reached the other 17 tabs.
  2. The 🏞️ Background tab is the biggest of them by far: 1,337 rows in 100 groups, 81% of them the three castles' rooms. In a place, a player sees 9 rows (the forest) to 50 (outside a castle), one under the other in a narrow column, the groups as dashed boxes.
  3. The proposal: one approach for every panel, nine rules (below): a box for each thing you can see or hear, named once; nesting shown by boxes inside boxes alone; everything laid out in plain view, in order (knolled); the thing shown, not just named; and every big tab a designer with the game as its first card, its boxes mirroring what is in the picture.
  4. Three before and afters: the castle's color themes (one decision in one place, its colors shown), the bubble themes (three cards, each with its bubble drawn), and a full-screen 🏞️ Background designer (the game as its first card; the place picked from pictures of the places; what stands in the place as a card each, with its picture).
  5. Found on the way: the house's 🧸 toys slider never shows (two rows share a key), and the bubble themes' on/off switches look the same on and off.

Four ideas

Knolling. Laying out the things you are working with, at right angles, grouped by kind, all in plain view. The word is said to come from Frank Gehry's furniture shop in 1987, where Andrew Kromelow squared up the tools that way. The artist Tom Sachs made "always be knolling" a rule of his studio: put away what you are not using, group what you are, and lay it out so that you can see all of it. For a panel: each thing's controls together, in its own place, aligned with its neighbours, nothing hidden in a list that scrolls past.

Boxes within boxes, with little visual noise. Sub-structure should be visible as structure: a part of a thing sits in a box inside that thing's box, and that is all the decoration it needs. No dashed borders, no repeated names, no instructions in a label.

Fractal organization. The same shapes at every scale: a tab is a box of boxes, a box is a box of rows, a row a line of chips. Learn one level and you can read them all; zooming in or out changes the scale, not the rules.

Mirror worlds. David Gelernter's Mirror Worlds (1991), subtitled "the day software puts the universe in a shoebox", imagines software models of the world that you can look into whole and then zoom into, part by part: a view he called topsight. The world designer is a small one already: the game's world as a grid of squares, each a picture of its place, and the game itself in a card beside it. A designer for each panel would mirror what that panel changes: the place, the friend, the music.

A global shift: the rules

  1. A box for each thing you can see or hear: a place, a thing standing in it, a part of the friend, a channel, a theme. Its name once, at its top, emoji first; nothing inside repeats it (no "🅱️ colour" inside theme B).
  2. Nesting by boxes alone: each level a shade lighter than the one it is in, no dashed lines; kinds told apart by a faint tint, as the music's strips and the world's squares are.
  3. Knolled: boxes in a grid, things of a kind the same size and lined up, everything a box holds in plain view. A 👁️ fold only for the big ones, remembered in the browser, as the world's view is.
  4. A row on one line: its emoji and two or three words, its control, its value, the music's knob as the model. What a label explains today ("% for each tenth of the picture", "0 = a blur") moves into a hint shown on pointing.
  5. Show the thing: a color as a swatch, a theme as what it draws (a bubble, a flag), a place as its picture, a thing standing in a place as its own picture.
  6. One decision, one place: a theme chosen where its colors are set, or its colors shown where it is chosen; a setting used in two tabs is one box shown in both, and one setting, not two.
  7. Mirror the picture: every big tab a designer that opens with the game as its first card; each box mirrors something in it; pointing at a box lights its thing in the game, and pointing at a thing in the game finds its box.
  8. The same at every scale: tab, designer, box, box inside it, row, chip; the side panel is the designer's boxes, smaller, not a different layout.
  9. Keep it fast: a box is built once and refreshed by what it shows, and show-when rules stay lookups (ticket 167: the Background tab's refresh once held swimming at 2 to 15 frames a second).

In code, three pieces carry all of it: a box (its name, its tint, its fold, its grid of what it holds), the row the music's knobs already are, and the designer's shell the two full screens share (the bar, ✕, the cleared page, the game card). TABS would say which boxes a tab has, and SHOWN_WHEN would show and hide whole boxes as it shows and hides rows now.

Found on the way

  • the house's 🧸 toys slider never shows. "toys" is the key of two rows (🧸 toys on the lawn, at the house; 🧸 things in the room, inside the tree house) and SHOWN_WHEN defines background.toys twice; the second (inside the tree house) wins, so at the house the slider is hidden, and inside the tree house both show, for the same setting (js/20-controls.js:359, 376, 821, 901)
  • the bubble themes' on/off shows nothing. see above (js/20-controls.js:100, 108, 515; style.css:186)
  • long labels with instructions in them. "🔭 smaller going north (% for each tenth of the picture)", "💀 skulls: biggest size (% of the usual; the smallest is the usual)", "… detail (0 = a blur, 100 = all of it)" (js/20-controls.js:318, 344, 487)
  • colour and color. the bubble rows say colour; the rest of the game color (js/20-controls.js:93-113)

What we got wrong on the way

  • Our first background mockup invented the forest's settings (clouds, trees, flowers, day and night): the forest has none of its own. The mockup is the lake now, with the lake's real five.
  • Our first castle mockup gave every room a theme; only rooms with doors of their own take one (castle N, at its defaults: the king's room alone).
