Then and now

How do Messed Up Dress Up, and the way it is being made, compare with the adventure-game engines and editors of the late 1980s and early 1990s (Sierra's AGI and SCI, LucasArts' SCUMM, and the Hugo games): what did they have that we have again, what made their making hard, which of those difficulties we meet again now, and which of ours are new? The game at 0.0.232; written from the sources listed at the end, and from the game's code and history (^ticket(295)).

Short answers.
  1. The machinery is the same, found again. A picture with invisible layers behind it: one saying what stands in front of what (Sierra's priority screen, in 12 bands; our objects, each with a depth) and one saying where one may walk (Sierra's control lines and control image, SCUMM's walk boxes; our collision boxes and back line). We came to each piece from M's and N's requests, not from the old engines.
  2. Our pictures are drawings in code, as AGI's were, not paintings scanned in as SCI1's were from 1990. So every part of a picture can be a setting and an object: placed, hidden, found by 🔬 Inspection.
  3. Their editors stood beside a compiled game; ours are the game. SCUMM's FLEM placed objects in rooms and MMUCUS spared scripters a rebuild; 🏞️ Background and 🗺️ World design change the picture the player is in, and a release is live in a minute: 232 versions since 2026-10-12.
  4. Of their eleven hard problems we meet 9 again. Memory and disks have become the browser's painting on slow computers; ports to the Amiga and the Mac have become differences between browsers (today's trap doors swung in Chrome and not in Firefox).
  5. New to our time: the designers are the players, the programmer works from their words in minutes, the game is its own editor, nothing is installed, and the game is a prototype for Hazel's livelits.
  6. Against Hugo's House of Horrors (1990): on par in the feel of moving through drawn places, ahead in making your own; behind in everything that made it a game: a goal, things to carry, puzzles, people who talk. What next is about that.
Editor's note (Matthew, 2026-10-18): "I appreciate getting reminded that we don't have all of the ingredients of a game, or even a game maker, yet. We need more ability to script events, add special items with special events attached, and write a story that the NP[C]s help create -- they are still just standins for that. Thank you."

What is new now

  • The designers are the players. M and N ask for a thing (a moss, a boulder lever, a bubble that bursts into smaller ones), and it is in the game that afternoon. In 1990 a design was decided by a designer, built for a year, and met players on floppy disks.
  • The programmer works from their words. An AI (Claude) writes the code from what Matthew relays, in plain words, and releases it; Matthew decides. The cost of trying an idea is minutes, so ideas get tried rather than argued about.
  • The game is its own editor. FLEM and MMUCUS were tools beside a compiled game; 🏞️ Background and 🗺️ World design are the running game, and a change shows at once in the picture the player is in.
  • Nothing to install. The game is a web page; a release is a push, live in about a minute, on any computer with a browser.
  • Every thing is a thing. Since today, everything a place draws is meant to be an object you can see in 🔬 Inspection, hide and place elsewhere (^ticket(284)); a picture's settings decide it exactly, seeds included (^ticket(274)).
  • It is a prototype. Dress Up is where Hazel's user-defined livelits are tried first: what works here, as the best experience when nothing stands in the way, is then built in Hazel.

Hugo, and "on par"

Matthew remembers playing Hugo in the mid 1990s: most likely Hugo's House of Horrors (1990) or a sequel [15] [16] [17]. His sense: "it feels like we are on par with that game play experience already".

On par in the feel of being somewhere drawn and moving through it, and past it in making your own. Behind it in what made it a game.

What next

  1. 1. Things you carry: an inventory, and using a thing on a thing (a key on a door, a bucket on a fire), the oldest machinery of these games.
  2. 2. Puzzles from what we have: levers, trap doors and boulders (^ticket(323)) wired to a goal in a place ("get the crown out of the trap room").
  3. 3. Depth and walking, drawn: 🔬 Inspection showing a place's depths and where one may walk as Sierra's priority and control screens did, and 🏞️ Background letting a designer paint them.
  4. 4. People who talk: the cooks, the witch and the healer answering, the player choosing what to say.
  5. 5. A beginning and an end: a world with a goal and a way to finish it, set in 🗺️ World design.
  6. 6. Rules: small scripts ("when the friend picks up the key, open the door"), in the game first, then as Hazel livelits, as SCUMM's scripts were to its rooms.
  7. 7. A world to share: a world saved as a file another player can open, as shareware once passed from hand to hand.

What the sources did not say

  • How SCUMM's walk boxes were drawn and stored: the sources found name them (ScummVM fixes them for the Commodore 64 Maniac Mansion) but do not describe the tools.
  • Sierra's own scanning pipeline for the painted VGA backgrounds: the sources describe it only in general (a 2015 trailer; an art director's biography).
  • The footnote engines above: named from memory, not checked.
